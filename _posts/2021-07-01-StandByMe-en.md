about_project: |-
  <h4>origins</h4>

  Stand by Me was born while we were still working on [Astro Pig](/-/games/astro-pig/), in the middle of the [Startup Rio](https://www.faperj.br/?id=239.6.9) acceleration program. We had just released [Cartomante](/-/games/cartomante/), in September 2020, and enjoyed the experience: we managed to produce a small game, with a cool concept, that got an interesting response for a first release made without much pretension. So our idea was to dedicate the next three months to another short, quick project, something we could also make from start to finish and release quickly.

  We spent a little over a month working on an idea, a little game that never came out, with the working title <b>Three Boss Fights</b> (TBF), affectionately nicknamed "Tribufu" (a brazilian slang for 'really ugly'). The concept was similar to another game we liked, <i>Just One Boss</i> - as you can tell from the names, it was basically the same thing, only with three boss fights in a row. The whole game revolved around making those bosses interesting: simple mechanics of rolling, hitting and waiting, and bosses with striking designs, each one representing a different challenge. A short scope, which we imagined we could produce quickly.

  We did get to make some really cool stuff there. We had concept art and narrative ideas to tie it all together (because, writer that I am, I try to squeeze a little bit of narrative into everything). It was also in Tribufu that we started developing v1 of Garoa's localization system, which later became quite robust in Astro Pig itself, showed up a little in Stand by Me, and has been used since conception in [RhythMania](/-/games/rhythmania/). And we had a gimmick I still love to this day: in Tribufu's New Game+, the bosses' title cards and battle blurbs, originally written in the [Theban alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theban_alphabet), would start showing up in the Latin alphabet, and the player could figure out the story of what was going on.

  After about a month of production, we decided not to continue, because the production pace wasn't fast enough for what we wanted. Knowing us, it would probably have come out through sheer stubbornness, but we really wanted something quicker to make and finish (which didn't really work out either, see below).

  <h4>two months that turned into eight</h4>

  That's when we picked up an [old prototype](https://raposodev.itch.io/sbm-project) by [Gabriel Raposo](https://raposodev.itch.io), inspired by the [ending of the anime Sarazanmai](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UWY2y4D1Qks) (which was called Stand By Me), an animation made of geometric shapes. The prototype was a simple magnetism puzzle in which you control a little ball indirectly: instead of pressing one way or the other to move it, the player activates magnets that guide it based on physics, and that's how you solve the puzzles.

  Since the project already had a foundation, an original concept, and Raposo had already programmed the basic physics, we thought: "let's just make this a bit more robust for release. It can't take more than two or three months." It ended up taking eight months. And, looking at the time and effort invested relative to the return, it wasn't our most "efficient" project. Still, we were very happy with the quality of the result. I feel like we squeezed blood from a stone, because, just like with Astro Pig, the concept itself imposed a lot of game design limitations. We tried to extract as many different forms of interaction as possible and create a fluid level design that wouldn't wear players out. I made over 200 levels myself; many (most) were discarded, and several only made it into the final game after a few rounds of revision, done in tandem.

  It was also with Stand by Me that some of Garoa's internal tropes as a team started to emerge, like the fact that we're very particular (euphemism) about certain artistic choices, especially colors. We had many, many discussions about the game's visuals. We wanted to rely less on our lead artist, Carol Sá, who was busy with another external project at the time, so we bet on doing a lot with shaders, without giving up on an art direction that tied everything together. Getting that whole lasagna of shaders and post-processing to work cohesively was a bigger technical challenge than expected, but we pulled it off.

  We also managed to keep a narrative thread throughout the game, which brought its own implementation challenges: we wanted to tell the story without getting in the way of the gameplay, so that it could be ignored entirely, but that it would also connect things for anyone interested - and help productize the game, so it wouldn't be just a "soulless" puzzle of geometric shapes. We also grew musically: Cartomante had one original track, and SBM had four.

  In the end, the game launched with seven "books" and over 100 stages, with "boss fights" (in quotes) at the end of each book and an entire book dedicated to a single boss fight (apparently we really did want to make boss fights - we even ended up using a chain mechanic that was in Tribufu), which ties together all the mechanics from the previous books.

  <h4>yes, you can be "too original"</h4>

  The result is a puzzle game, ~sort of~. Physics-based, also sort of. SBM is unique to the point of being hard to fit into the Steam algorithm - and it was with it that we learned, in depth, how much tags can work for or against a game when it comes to sales. To this day we have a hard time fitting Stand by Me into festivals, showcases, events and lists of similar games, because it is, for better and for worse, maybe just too unique. SEO doesn't help either: <i>Stand by Me</i> is a movie, a song and a common English expression, so it's hard to find anything about the game these days (a game's name is hugely important for discoverability, I recommend investing a lot of time in it).

  But we like it. It has its own charisma and charm and, like the others, it had its fair share of official selections and awards (even if fewer than the rest). Anyone on the outside would look at this project - us included, with today's eyes - and say it was never going to come out, and we made it come out, while we were still dragging our feet on Astro Pig's development. It's [available on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1484600/Stand_By_Me/), and it's one of the Garoa games I most often catch myself reinstalling to replay, speedrunning it from start to finish.

  We had so many unrealized ideas for it that Stand by Me was one of the only Garoa games (as of this post's publication, the only one) to get a [free post-launch update](https://steamcommunity.com/games/1484600/announcements/detail/4186652357024862361), with skills, unlockable abilities. They don't interfere with the base game at all: you can completely ignore the bonus stages that unlock the skills, but whoever plays those stages and unlocks them gets a whole range of new, creative interactions with every other stage in the game.

  It was a great outlet for our creativity: an intriguing game, both visually and mechanically, and people who play it have fun. It's just strange enough that few people look at it in the store and decide to play it. That became a lesson for our next projects: the visual presentation and the hook, the concept, need to win over whoever is looking within a few seconds. In that respect, as much as I think Stand by Me is a good, well-executed game, it falls short.

  In terms of production, we also delivered a good project, but it didn't fulfill its original purpose, which was to be something quick, and it ended up wearing the team down a bit along the way.

  While I could throw stones at this "middle child" of mine all day long, I really love the adventures of Pico and his friends, these charismatic little balls in a trippy magnetic world, and I think we did a good job weaving in aphorisms and parables that speak directly to the stages you're playing. I hope Pico's journey brings at least some reflection to whoever plays it.
